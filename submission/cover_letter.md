Dear Editors,

I am submitting a Research Article, *Street slope and reported property crime in nine
United States cities: terrain measurement, target exposure, and tests of candidate
mechanisms*, for consideration at PLOS ONE.

Haberman and Kelsay (2021, *Journal of Quantitative Criminology*) reported that
robberies fall with street grade in Cincinnati, and closed by naming three possible
explanations — physical cost, escape difficulty, and lower street usage — without
testing between them. That untested sentence is the starting point for this paper. I
extend the outcome from robbery to property crime, the setting from one city to nine,
and I build a design in which the candidate explanations make different predictions.

The contribution is not the association itself, which has precedents in Breetzke
(2012), Kim and Wo (2023), and two 2026 bicycle-theft studies. It is the combination of
a standardized cross-city pipeline, a within-place offense-type contrast that isolates
the cost of removing property, direct target-count exposure denominators in place of
census housing, and an unusually detailed measurement audit. Across nine cities the
pooled estimate is −6.89% per degree of slope, but with a prediction interval for a new
city running from −13.7% to +0.5%, and I am explicit that the magnitude does not
transport.

I want to be equally explicit about what the paper does not establish. It is
observational; block-group fixed effects remove between-neighborhood variation and
nothing else, and I do not claim that gradient deters offenders. Of the four candidate
mechanisms tested, one — that the association is driven by the metabolic cost of
carrying stolen goods — is bounded by an equivalence test. The others are narrowed
rather than eliminated. The paper ends with the mechanism unidentified, which I judged
more useful than supplying a fifth explanation I could not test.

The manuscript reports several analyses that did not work, four classifier defects
found by audit, a post-hoc terrain threshold that I demoted to a sensitivity analysis
after external review, and one extension retracted and re-run after its models proved
not to have converged. All are itemized in S1 Appendix. The headline estimates are also
reproduced with independent software (statsmodels), agreeing to 8.3 × 10⁻⁷.

Analysis code, the aggregated analytic panels, all result tables and the classifier
validation set are publicly archived at
https://github.com/walk-the-program/Street-Slope-and-Reported-Property-Crime.

This manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, has not been previously submitted
to PLOS, and has no related submissions. I have no competing interests and received no
funding for this work. I am an independent researcher and the sole author.

Suggested Academic Editors would be handling editors with expertise in environmental
criminology, crime and place, or spatial analysis of urban form. I oppose no reviewers.

Thank you for your consideration.
